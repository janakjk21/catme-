# Research Question Audit and Validation Plan

## Audit of the original research brief

The original questions cover the right territory, but they were too wide for one research pass. Many list ideas (audiences, features, technologies, monetization) before stating which decision each answer changes. Desk research can show current products, their listings and aggregate category context; it cannot prove CatMe’s desirability, customer understanding, likeness acceptance, return rate or willingness to pay.

The research should be ordered around the riskiest assumptions. Ask about past behaviour before showing the product. Keep photo creator, companion game, cozy room game and memorial use as competing hypotheses. Separate stated interest from observed use. Use a prototype only for the question its fidelity can answer. Follow a positive desk signal with a falsifying test.

## Ten priority questions

| Rank | Research question / decision | Assumption | Evidence and method | What would disconfirm it |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who has a recurring need that CatMe could serve? | A real cat relationship creates unmet digital entertainment/identity need. | Past-behaviour interviews with current owners, cozy players, people without pets, creators. | People like the idea only after prompting and already have satisfactory alternatives. |
| 2 | What is the primary product: companion, room game, creator or memory tool? | One is a clear reason to return. | Randomized concept/storyboard choice plus follow-up use test. | No concept wins, or photo output is wanted only once. |
| 3 | Does personal identity improve the experience over a high-quality standard cat? | Resemblance gives lasting value. | Compare same interaction with personalized and authored cats; owner recognition. | Identity has little effect on preference/return, or close inspection lowers trust. |
| 4 | What repeated behaviour is actually enjoyable? | Cat autonomy plus a few inputs makes a good session. | Unmoderated phone test of 1 polished interaction; observation and replay. | Players tap once, wait, become confused, or ask for goals/content the prototype lacks. |
| 5 | What visual direction supports identity and animation? | Warm semi-realistic room is right. | Phone-size identical clips/stills and preference/comprehension tasks. | Stylized direction is more recognizable/readable or warm room feels generic. |
| 6 | Which cat creation method meets likeness and mobile quality? | Photo-generated 3D is feasible and accepted. | Representative benchmark, blind ratings, model and device metrics. | Rig/artifact failure or cost/latency overwhelms value. |
| 7 | What makes people return after first creation? | Memories/discovery/bond create natural continuation. | Several-day diary/use test with no forced reminders. | No voluntary return or users only want export/share. |
| 8 | Which things are worth paying for? | Personal cat, interactive objects or room packs have value. | Forced-choice realistic bundles at prices, followed by small real test only when store-ready. | Strong stated intent vanishes when choosing between free options and offers. |
| 9 | Can unit economics work for a small team? | Net payer value covers acquisition, generation and content. | Actual provider quotes/invoices + funnel/cohort test and scenario model. | Cost per accepted cat or ongoing content exceeds net receipts under plausible conversion. |
| 10 | Can the brand serve personal play and memory without harm/confusion? | A single warm cat brand can do both. | Separate concept tests and interviews, including opt-in memory users. | Players perceive trivialization, misleading claims or conflicting expectations. |

## Question quality improvements

Replace “What features should we add?” with “At which observed moment does the current session fail, for which user, and what smallest change could address it?”

Replace “Would you use/buy this?” with “When did you last pay for a cat game/photo or keep a pet memory? What did you choose instead? Here are concrete alternatives; which would you choose at these prices?”

Replace “Does it look good?” with “What do you notice first? What do you think you can do? Which cat reads as the same individual after it turns and moves? What feels off?”

Replace “Would you return?” with a short longitudinal test that observes whether the person voluntarily opens the app and what they do next.

## Primary study plan (not yet conducted)

### Study A: Discovery interviews

- Start with 12–18 adult participants across 3–4 segments, six or so per priority segment if recruitment permits; continue until patterns and meaningful contradictions stabilize. This is a qualitative sample, not a population estimate.
- Recruit US participants first through cat-owner communities, cozy-game communities, and general mobile gamers. Include people who do not want a pet or dislike virtual pets. Avoid only recruiting enthusiastic CatMe followers. Record state/region, age band, cat ownership, mobile-game habits, and recruitment source so a convenience sample is not mistaken for a US population estimate. Add a smaller UK comparison only after the first US patterns are clear.
- Ask about recent cat-related app/game/photo behaviour, last purchase, last shared moment, what they do when away from cat, which apps they have deleted, and why. Ask “tell me about the last time…” rather than “would you like…”.
- Only after behaviour questions, show 2–3 neutral concepts. Randomize order; do not introduce the founder’s story or current feature list.
- Record verbatim statements with consent and separate quote from interviewer inference. Do not collect personal pet photos in early discovery.

### Study B: First-session phone usability

- Five to eight target users per iteration is a practical initial usability round, not a statistical result.
- Give the device without instructions. Tasks: find what cat is doing; try to get its attention; play with one object; capture/keep a moment; leave and reopen.
- Observe first tap, hesitation, camera gestures, ignored touch, confusion, delight, frustration, and whether they can explain cause/effect. Let participant control the phone.
- Ask afterward what they expected, what they would remove, and what they would show someone. Keep interviewers neutral.
- Run on physical devices and build type appropriate for release-like input. Editor mouse success is not phone validation.

### Study C: Likeness and style

- Obtain explicit photo/model processing consent. Provide local-only alternative; explain vendor transfer and deletion first.
- Compare generated and authored/customized cats with same behavior and camera. Ask the owner to identify the cat and name distinguishing features before revealing the method.
- Show front, side, walk, turn, and action. Include poor cases, not just showcase outputs.
- Separate “looks like my cat,” “looks like a cat,” “looks good,” “moves naturally,” and “I would use this again.”

### Study D: Return and offer validation

- Invite participants to use the prototype for 7–14 days without daily reminder pressure; log voluntary visits, actions, and qualitative notes with consent.
- Afterwards present concrete free/paid bundles in randomized order, with actual visible contents and prices. This is stated choice, not purchase proof.
- Once product and billing are ready, test a small opt-in purchase with transparent refund/support and no false scarcity. Track conversions, refunds, generation acceptance, and support cost.

## Analysis and decision rules

Maintain an evidence ledger. Code observations by segment, trigger, job, desired outcome, alternative, barrier, language and confidence. Search for counterexamples. A pattern heard from several participants is a direction to test, not a prevalence statistic.

Prototype maps should say: **learning goal → unknown → riskiest assumption → prototype fidelity → participant → task → observation → decision**. IDEO recommends selecting prototypes based on learning goals and unknowns. [Method](https://www.designkit.org/methods/determine-what-to-prototype.html)

Use numerical thresholds only when they are explicitly proposed for a decision. Example proposed rule: if fewer than a clear majority of a small tested target cohort can explain how to get a cat response without help, iterate controls before adding content. The exact threshold should be set before seeing results and reported as a small-sample rule, not an industry standard.

## Research findings translated into testable design decisions

The desk evidence supports a small set of **test propositions**, not user findings:

1. **Contingency may create connection:** after a tap/call/toy movement, show the cat noticing the actual stimulus before choosing what to do. Compare this with an immediate generic animation. Observe whether players can describe cause and response and whether they smile, repeat, or take a photo.
2. **Subtlety needs staging:** real-cat body language relies on small changes and context; phone screens shrink them. Compare natural-scale motion with slightly exaggerated head/ear/tail and anticipation. Ask viewers to identify “curious / relaxed / playful / done” and test misreadings. Do not animate permanent smiling or infer mood as a factual pet trait.
3. **A real ending may be more rewarding than endless chasing:** cat play sources emphasize a stalk/chase/pounce/catch sequence. Compare a toy session that ends in a catch with an endless laser chase. Observe frustration, completion, and voluntary restart. Always give the cat a catch or clear settled ending.
4. **A cohesive room may beat more furniture:** compare an uncluttered behavioral stage, aspirational cozy room, and owner-like room. Ask what the cat is doing, what can be touched, what each object contributes, and what they would change. Count comprehension errors, not only stated preference.
5. **Identity and fun are separate axes:** use the same interaction with (a) a high-quality generic cat and (b) photo-informed identity. Ask both “does it feel like this cat?” and “would you play this interaction again?” If identity lifts recognition but not repeat use, keep photo creation as an optional creator/keepsake layer; don't call it a game loop.
6. **Calm and comedy may fit if humor arises from cat behavior:** compare a quiet observation clip with a near-miss/pawing clip using the same room and cat. Ask what felt funny, fake, stressful, or affectionate. Avoid human slapstick as the default.

### Proposed first three playtests (exact decisions)

| Experiment | Material | Participant task | Measures | Decision changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Does the cat feel present? | Same 15-second moment in three visual treatments: current warm cream/oak 3D; illustrated/storybook; toy-like. | B. | C. Is personalization worth the friction? | Generic authored cat, customized markings, photo-derived still/model, all doing identical toy action. ### Interview guide focused on the desired emotional connection

Use with adults in US first and a smaller UK comparison; purposively include current cat owners and a contrast group that plays cozy games but does not own cats. Ask before showing concepts:

- “Tell me about the last small thing your cat did that made you laugh or stop what you were doing.” What happened just before it? What did you do afterward?
- “When do you most often take a photo or video of your cat? What do you do with it later?” Can you show a recent example if comfortable, without uploading it to CatMe?
- “Tell me about a cat-related app or game you last opened. What did you do? What made you come back or stop?”
- “When you're away from your cat, if at all, what do you do that involves them?” Don't suggest the game as an answer.
- “What would feel wrong or uncomfortable about a digital version of your actual cat?”
- After viewing a neutral concept: “What do you think happens in this app? What would you do first? What do you expect would cost money? What would make you close it?”
- Ask them to choose between a familiar cat, a new authored cat, a decorated room, and a short photo/video maker, including “none.” Ask why and what they would actually install this week.

Do not ask “Would you use a game that brings your cat to life?” because it flatters the concept and assumes its value. Do not frame grief or emotional need as a conversion lever. For UK participants, keep the same interview but note vocabulary, store expectations, and price currency; don't pool raw answers as one homogeneous market.

### Small-scale economics validation

The stated goal is on the order of 10–15 people/day or 100 people/month, not mass-market scale. The research should therefore measure whether a small set of players becomes strongly satisfied and willing to support the product. Define which goal means visits, new users, or active people before analytics; 100 downloads/month and 100 active users/month are different outcomes.

For an early opt-in cohort, track individually: first-minute completion, interaction replay, day 1/7/30 voluntary return, photo setup completion, likeness accept/reject, support minutes, and actual purchases/refunds. With only tens of players, report exact counts and anonymized observations (e.g., 4/12), not percentages presented as market truth. A dozen interviews can reveal reasons; they cannot estimate the proportion of US/UK players who will buy.

Offer test should ask users to allocate a limited hypothetical budget across: (1) full one-time CatMe experience, (2) personal-cat creation, (3) one behavior-changing toy/room pack, (4) optional cosmetic-only pack, (5) free/no purchase. Then validate with an actual small transaction only after the feature and refund/support path work. A click on a purchase card is weaker than a paid purchase; stated intent is weaker than retained use.

## Additional required workstreams

### Trust, photo privacy, and continuity

Before any real photo generation test, draw the data path from picker → local file → upload → provider → output → local/server cache → deletion. For each hop, record data type, purpose, access, retention, security owner, and deletion method. Use only a test photo for technical QA unless the owner explicitly consents; do not upload a participant's photo to a third-party generator without a clear vendor disclosure and separate consent. In prototype sessions, test whether a person can (a) understand upload before selecting, (b) skip and still play, (c) cancel, (d) reject/delete an unwanted result, and (e) explain whether the image remains stored. Record trust confusion and choice behavior, not only task success.

Run continuity cases on actual iOS and Android builds: kill/reopen; app update; device backup/restore; reinstall; generated asset replaced; low storage/cache eviction; remove source image; delete CatMe data; export a moment. Confirm what survives in each case before writing a promise. The current code uses `Application.persistentDataPath` for the core save and companion journal, and caches the active generated model separately; restore/uninstall/device migration behavior is still unknown. Decide which state is durable user content and which is rebuildable cache.

For UK launch, assess whether the game is likely to be accessed by children and map relevant Children's Code standards. For US launch, assess whether the service is child-directed or has actual knowledge of under-13 personal-data collection, and review the amended COPPA rule. Do not make an age-gate a substitute for product-specific analysis. Use legal counsel before launch; research notes identify design questions, not legal conclusions.

### Accessibility and emotional-comfort test

Include at least one pass with sound off, one with reduced motion enabled, one with larger text, and one with VoiceOver/TalkBack enabled for menus and onboarding. Ask players to identify the cat's response and complete the first interaction without relying on color alone, tiny targets, or hearing a cue. Audit tap targets, contrast, captions/visual equivalents for sound, camera motion, repetitive idle motion, and whether the player can interrupt an animation. Compare full and reduced motion to ensure the cat remains emotionally legible, not simply static. Recruit disabled participants for a dedicated round if the product advances; do not treat a developer checklist as user validation.

Also test whether the proposed silly/jiggly beats feel warm, funny, uncanny, or stressful. Ask what specific movement triggered the reaction. Separate photorealism preference from readable action and likeness.

### Sustainable production and support test

Time the vertical slice from brief to shippable-quality behavior: animation, code, staging, UI, sound, device QA, store capture, accessibility pass and fixes. Record actual iteration/rework time and future maintenance owner. Before adding each feature, use this small-project gate: it must improve the core moment, add a reusable behavior, or be explicitly selected by a user test; estimate one-time plus recurring work; and show the interaction in an honest demo. A content roadmap is not sustainable if one creator cannot maintain its promised cadence.

### Discovery experiment appropriate for roughly 100 people/month

First define whether the owner target is 100 new installs, 100 monthly active people, or 100 paying customers. Use a small source log (store search, creator link, direct community, friend referral, press/blog, other) and a truthful landing/store page. At very low traffic, do not declare an A/B winner from a handful of installs. Use individual interviews and same-screen concept comparisons first; treat platform experiments as exploratory until the console estimates enough volume. Track qualified impressions → product page visits → installs → first complete moment → voluntary return → purchase/refund, with one source tag per path. Report counts and confidence honestly. Never count page views as active players or a store install as emotional fit.

US and UK search terms should be observed separately. Capture exact suggestions, spelling and query context by storefront/region and date; test whether the searcher expects a game, photo effect, simulator, or memory product. Store page promise must match actual gameplay. Only after meaningful impressions should an icon, first screenshot or promise test be used to tune acquisition.

### Longitudinal success and stopping metrics

Pre-register a compact product scorecard before a public test:

| Question | Early measure | Interpretation limit |
|---|---|---|
| Did first use deliver the promise? | Count who completes one understandable cat moment without help; note first confusion. | Small cohort is diagnostic, not a universal usability score. |
| Did the moment create warmth/fun? | Unprompted description and replay/save choice; code exact words and negative reactions. | Do not treat smiles or “cute” as retention. |
| Does identity matter? | Owner recognition reasons and preference versus identical authored-cat interaction. | Recognition does not automatically prove repeated use. |
| Does it earn a return? | Voluntary D1/D7/D30 returns and what action they choose, separated by source and cohort. | Small counts; publish numerator/denominator and avoid benchmark comparisons. |
| Is there a sustainable purchase? | Real purchases, refunds, accepted result, support time and contribution margin. | Stated intention, taps and hypothetical allocation are not sales. |
| Is the maker able to sustain it? | Hours per slice/release, defects, provider failures, support minutes, backlog. | Include unpaid founder time and content obligations. |

Agree in advance what result would make CatMe (a) continue the current direction, (b) simplify to authored cats, (c) pivot to a photo/keepsake creator, or (d) stop adding features. The small audience goal makes customer-level depth, voluntary return, and maker sustainability more useful than category-wide TAM.
